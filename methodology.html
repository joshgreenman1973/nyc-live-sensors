The city that sensors see (and hear and feel)Methodology

How this page works

This page documents every data source behind The city that sensors see (and hear and feel), how each number is computed, how often it refreshes and what to be careful about. The page was built with AI assistance in July 2026. There is no database and no server: your browser reads most feeds directly from the agencies, and a scheduled script fills in the rest.

How data reaches the page

Feeds fall into two groups:

  • Direct feeds. Sources that allow cross-origin requests are fetched by your browser, live, on a timer of one to five minutes per feed. Nothing passes through any intermediate server.
  • Snapshot feeds. Sources that block cross-origin browser requests (the New York Independent System Operator, the National Data Buoy Center, the Metropolitan Transportation Authority, NYC Ferry, the traffic-camera directory, Lime and Bird) and the one too large to read on every visit (AirNow's air-quality file) are fetched by a script that runs every 10 minutes and writes data/snapshot.json. The script runs inside one long GitHub Actions job that loops for about five and a half hours and then starts its successor; from October 2026 GitHub's own scheduler started only three or four runs a day of the original 15-minute schedule, which left these tiles hours stale. Tiles fed this way are usually 10 to 15 minutes behind; each shows its own "as of" time.

Each tile's source line links to the agency's or operator's own page for that instrument or feed, where its fuller record usually lives.

Every reading carries its "as of" time. When the latest reading is older than that feed's usual rhythm, the line says "Lagging" and the rule above the reading turns yellow; when the feed cannot be read at all, it says "No signal." The paragraph at the top counts how many feeds are current, lagging or quiet, and the opening sentence is built from whichever vehicle feeds (subway, buses, aircraft, ships) are current.

The map

The map plots every sensor that has a published location, colored by domain and toggleable by layer. Fixed instruments (tide gauges, current meters, the wave buoy, stream gauges, weather stations, cameras, bike docks) sit at their installed coordinates; moving things (aircraft, ferries) appear at their last transmitted position. Traffic sensors draw as their actual road segments, colored by the latest speed. Clicking a camera dot loads that camera's live frame.

  • Camera coordinates come from a data/stations.json file the snapshot script rewrites at most once a day (the hardware doesn't move; this keeps the every-10-minutes commits small). Aircraft and ferry positions come from the snapshot, so they are up to about 10 minutes old; the ferry popup says so.
  • The traffic feed's coordinate strings are dirty — truncated pairs and dropped minus signs. Points outside a plausible New York box are discarded and lines are split where consecutive points jump implausibly far, so a small number of segments render partially.
  • Subway trains are counted on the board but not mapped: the public train feed reports positions by track segment and stop, not coordinates. The base map is CARTO's dark tiles over OpenStreetMap data.

Source-by-source detail

TileSource and endpointRefreshNotes and caveats
Surface weather National Weather Service observations API: api.weather.gov/stations/KNYC|KLGA|KJFK/observations/latest Browser, every 5 min; stations report roughly hourly KNYC is the Central Park station. Airport sensors (Automated Surface Observing Systems) are the gold standard; the API occasionally returns null temperature fields, which the page skips.
A weather station in every borough New York State Mesonet, run by the University at Albany: api.nysmesonet.org/data/index.php/geojson/nysm/latest, stations MANH, BKLN, QUEE, BRON and STAT Browser, every 5 min; stations report every 5 min Research-grade stations, one per borough, so the tile can show how temperature and wind differ across the city rather than at three airports. Wind is the 5-minute average; the higher figure in a range is the peak gust. Readings older than three hours are dropped. Mesonet data may not be redistributed except by media users or under an agreement; this page uses it as a media user, with credit to the network.
Lightning New York State Mesonet lightning service: api.nysmesonet.org/data/index.php/lightning/list/<start>/latest for the five-minute batch times, then lightning/get/<time> for each batch's strikes Browser, every 5 min; batches every 5 min The page reads the past hour's twelve batches and counts strikes within 25 miles of City Hall, measured as straight-line distance. Each strike comes with a location and a time, nothing about its strength or whether it reached the ground. Coverage runs across New York State and somewhat past its borders, so "none anywhere the network covers" means none in that region, not none in the country. The Mesonet's own weather stations do not detect lightning, and the Mesonet does not say which detection network the strikes come from; the leading candidate is a commercial network whose owner restricts redistribution. The map shows strikes within about 90 miles.
Weather radar NEXRAD base-reflectivity tiles composited by the Iowa Environmental Mesonet: mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/cache/tile.py/.../nexrad-n0q-900913/, over a CARTO dark basemap Every 5 min The radar covering the city is KOKX in Upton, Suffolk County. Tiles are a national composite; the panel crops to the region around the city. Transparent tiles mean no echoes.
Satellite view GOES-East (GOES-19) Advanced Baseline Imager, northeast-sector GeoColor composite from NOAA's image server, cdn.star.nesdis.noaa.gov Browser, every 5 min; the satellite scans about that often A geostationary imager 22,236 miles over the equator. GeoColor is true color by day; at night it blends infrared cloud imagery with a static city-lights layer.
Low-orbit pass Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) aboard NOAA-20, served as true-color tiles by NASA's Global Imagery Browse Services: gibs.earthdata.nasa.gov Browser, hourly check; the satellite passes about 1:30 p.m. and imagery publishes a few hours later The one public satellite view meant to show the city itself rather than its weather — about 375 meters per pixel from 512 miles up. The panel shows today's pass once processed, otherwise the most recent available day (labeled). No public satellite feed of the city is live at street scale: geostationary satellites are too far away for fine detail, and the daily high-resolution constellations (Planet, Maxar, Airbus) sell their imagery rather than publish it.
Aircraft overhead adsb.lol API, read by the 10-minute snapshot robot: api.adsb.lol/v2/point/40.7128/-74.0060/30. Until November 2026 this tile read airplanes.live directly; that service now refuses anonymous requests, and adsb.lol does not let browsers read it, so the reading is up to about 10 minutes old. Snapshot robot, every 15 min Community-run network of Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast receivers. Counts aircraft not reporting "ground" status within 30 nautical miles of City Hall. Helicopter count uses the transponder's self-reported category (A7, rotorcraft), which some helicopters leave unset — treat it as a floor.
Air quality EPA AirNow's public reporting-area file, files.airnowtech.org/airnow/today/reportingarea.dat, rows for the "New York City Region" (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation monitors); plus the Open-Meteo air-quality API (Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service model) Snapshot, every 10 min; monitors report hourly. Model read by the browser every 15 min The headline is measured: the state's monitors' latest hourly air-quality index for the city, which AirNow posts 30 to 60 minutes after the hour ends. The index is the worse of the pollutants reported, usually ozone and fine particles. AirNow's API requires a key, but this public file does not; it is 1.3 MB, so the robot reads it and keeps only the city's rows. The Copernicus model's estimate for Lower Manhattan sits below as a second opinion, and takes over, labeled as a model, if the AirNow reading can't be loaded.
Harbor tides NOAA Center for Operational Oceanographic Products and Services: stations 8518750 (the Battery) and 8516945 (Kings Point), api.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov Browser, every 5 min; sensors report every 6 min Heights are in feet above mean lower low water, the standard chart datum. Next high/low times come from NOAA's own harmonic predictions. The Battery gauge is one of the longest-running instruments in the city, in service since 1920.
Harbor currents NOAA New York and New Jersey Harbor Physical Oceanographic Real-Time System current meters n06010 (Kill Van Kull, buoy LB14) and n07010 (Newark Bay entrance, buoy LB18), api.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov, product currents, shallowest bin Browser, every 6 min; meters report every 6 min Acoustic Doppler meters on navigation buoys measure how fast the water near the surface is moving and which way. The direction is where the water is going, not where it comes from. Speeds are in knots (1 knot is about 1.15 mph). These meters serve pilots bringing ships to Port Newark and Elizabeth; the harbor's other current meters were not reporting when this was built.
Wind on the harbor NOAA water-level stations 8530973 (Robbins Reef), 8519532 (Mariners Harbor) and 8516945 (Kings Point), products wind, air_temperature and air_pressure Browser, every 6 min; stations report every 6 min Wind direction is where the wind comes from. NOAA reports knots; the page converts to miles per hour. The Battery station measures air temperature and pressure but not wind, so it is not in this tile.
Stream and tide gauges United States Geological Survey instantaneous values service, queried by the five boroughs' county codes, parameter 00065 (gage height); a second query asks for water temperature (00010), dissolved oxygen (00300) and turbidity (63680) Browser, every 10 min; gauges report every 6 to 15 min The set of gauges is discovered live from the county query, so new deployments appear automatically. The gauges measure stream stage on creeks and rivers, not harbor tide (NOAA covers that). The query also returns gauges that have stopped transmitting, some still carrying the agency's -999,999 no-data code from an equipment fault; the tile counts only real readings from the last three hours. The same rule applies to the water-quality query; as of November 2026 the only sonde reporting live is the Bronx River at the New York Botanical Garden (a Rockaway Inlet sonde last reported in 2019). Dissolved oxygen below about 5 mg/L stresses fish; turbidity is cloudiness, in formazin nephelometric units.
Wave buoy National Data Buoy Center station 44065, ndbc.noaa.gov/data/realtime2/44065.txt Snapshot, every 10 min; buoy reports hourly Moored at the New York Harbor entrance about 15 miles south of Breezy Point. "Significant wave height" is the average of the highest third of waves, the standard oceanographic measure.
Ships in the harbor Automatic Identification System transponders, heard by aisstream.io's network of volunteer shore receivers and relayed through a small Cloudflare Worker (the same relay that powers the harbor-traffic project; the stream key stays inside the Worker) Browser listens to the stream for about 25 seconds, every 5 min Counts vessels heard during the listening window, so slow-transmitting anchored ships can be undercounted; large commercial vessels transmit every few seconds. "Underway" means faster than half a knot.
Traffic speed sensors NYC Department of Transportation real-time traffic speed detectors, via NYC Open Data dataset i4gi-tjb9 Browser, every 5 min; sensors report every few minutes These sensors sit on highways, bridges and tunnels — not local streets — so the average describes arterial traffic, not a crosstown block. Only the newest transmission is counted: readings within 30 minutes of the newest one in the feed. The city's copy of this feed sometimes stops updating for hours or days; when it does, the tile is marked lagging and names the day of its last update, and the map's road popups carry their reading's date. Borough averages are unweighted means across sensors.
Traffic cameras NYC Department of Transportation camera network, webcams.nyctmc.org Camera list refreshed with the snapshot; images pulled live every 9 seconds as the panel rotates, or on demand when a camera is clicked The automatic tour cycles through a geographic spread of online cameras; the mini-map beside the frame plots all of them, and clicking any dot loads that camera's live image. Images are typically a few seconds to a minute old at the source. Camera locations refresh daily via data/stations.json.
Citi Bike docks Citi Bike general bikeshare feed specification: gbfs.citibikenyc.com/gbfs/en/station_status.json Browser, every 3 min; feed updates about every minute Totals sum every station in the system, which includes Jersey City and Hoboken stations (a few percent of the system).
Shared scooters + e-bikes Each operator's general bikeshare feed: Veo cluster-prod.veoride.com/api/shares/name/nyc|nyq/gbfs/free_bike_status (read by the browser); Lime data.lime.bike/api/partners/v2/gbfs/new_york/ and Bird mds.bird.co/gbfs/v2/public/new-york/ (read by the snapshot robot, because they can't be read from a browser) Veo in the browser, every 5 min; Lime and Bird via snapshot, every 10 min Counts vehicles parked, charged enough to rent and not reserved; disabled and reserved ones are left out of the headline. The operators' feeds say whether each vehicle is a scooter or a bike; Veo's fleet is mostly seated e-bikes. Lime's feed includes a handful of vehicles outside the city, which are dropped. Only Veo's positions are mapped; the robot keeps counts, not positions, for Lime and Bird to keep its every-10-minutes commits small. The feeds publish anonymous vehicle IDs that change after each trip, not riders.
Bridge clearance NOAA Physical Oceanographic Real-Time System air-gap sensors: stations 8517986 (Verrazzano-Narrows) and 8519461 (Bayonne Bridge) Browser, every 5 min; sensors report every 6 min Microwave sensors under the bridge decks measure the actual air gap for ships, which rises and falls with the tide. These exist so ultra-large container ships know whether they fit.
Bike + walk counters NYC Department of Transportation automated bicycle and pedestrian counters, via NYC Open Data datasets ct66-47at (counts) and 6up2-gnw8 (sensor locations) Browser, every 15 min; counters bin in 15-minute intervals but reach the portal hours behind The tile sums the 24 hours ending at the newest posted bin (not the 24 hours ending now, which would undercount while the portal lags) and shows that bin's time. Only a handful of the counters tally pedestrians; the tile says how many. This is the least "live" feed on the board — the counters report continuously but the public copy lags a few hours, and the tile allows 10 hours before marking it lagging.
Buses reporting MTA Bus Time SIRI vehicle-monitoring feed, bustime.mta.info Browser, every 3 min; positions update continuously Uses a public, per-key rate-limited Bus Time key (the same one published in the companion bus-tracker project). Counts vehicles transmitting a position; buses deadheading or with dead trackers are invisible.
Elevators + escalators Metropolitan Transportation Authority current-outage feed for subway elevators and escalators Browser, every 5 min The feed mixes equipment out of service now with planned shutdowns scheduled for coming days and weeks (flagged as upcoming), which usually outnumber the current outages several times over. The headline counts only the current ones; the scheduled ones get their own row. "On accessible routes" counts outages the MTA flags as affecting Americans with Disabilities Act accessibility.
Commuter trains Long Island Rail Road and Metro-North real-time feeds (General Transit Feed Specification real-time) Snapshot, every 10 min Counts trains present in each railroad's feed; where a feed carries trip updates but not vehicle positions, active trips stand in for trains.
Subway trains reporting Metropolitan Transportation Authority real-time subway feeds (eight General Transit Feed Specification real-time feeds covering all lines) and the subway-alerts feed Snapshot, every 10 min Counts trains transmitting a vehicle position. This is "trains the signal system is reporting," which can differ slightly from trains physically in motion; a position message can persist briefly after a trip ends. Alert count includes planned service changes currently in effect.
NYC Ferry vessels NYC Ferry real-time vehicle-position feed Snapshot, every 10 min Counts boats transmitting positions; overnight the number drops to zero or near zero, which is real, not an outage, and the tile says so between midnight and 6 a.m.
Seismographs United States Geological Survey earthquake catalog, fed by the Lamont-Doherty cooperative seismographic network's stations around the region: earthquake.usgs.gov/fdsnws/event/1/query Browser, every 30 min Counts cataloged earthquakes within 300 kilometers of City Hall over the trailing 30 days; epicenters appear on the map. Most months the count is a small number of tiny magnitude-1-to-2 events; the tile shows the instruments are listening.
Upstate reservoirs Department of Environmental Protection daily reservoir-levels page (the agency's open-data version of this dataset stopped updating in October 2025, so the snapshot reads the public page itself) Snapshot, hourly check; the page updates daily with a one-to-two-day lag Percent of total system capacity, with the normal value for the date. Page scraping is inherently fragile; if the format changes the tile goes stale rather than guessing.
PATH trains Port Authority of New York and New Jersey RidePATH arrivals feed: panynj.gov/bin/portauthority/ridepath.json Snapshot, every 10 min The feed publishes upcoming arrivals per station rather than train positions, so the tile counts arrivals posted for the next 30 minutes — a service-level reading, not a train census. Snapshot timing means it can be up to 20 minutes behind.
Power outages Con Edison outage-map data feed, summary file Snapshot, every 10 min; the source updates every 15 to 30 min Customers without power and separate outage events across Con Edison's roughly 3.6 million customers. The Rockaways are served by PSEG Long Island and are not included. On a normal day a few hundred customers are dark somewhere.
Geomagnetic field NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center: planetary K index from its worldwide ground-magnetometer network, plus the current storm scales — services.swpc.noaa.gov Browser, every 30 min; the index updates every 3 hours (estimated more often) Not a New York City sensor, but the geomagnetic conditions it measures act on the city's grid, pipelines and satellite navigation. Kp below 3 is quiet; 5 or higher is a storm with a G-scale rating.
Electric demand New York Independent System Operator real-time actual load, mis.nyiso.com/public/csv/pal/, zone N.Y.C. Snapshot, every 10 min; source updates about every 5 min The N.Y.C. zone (load zone J) covers the five boroughs. The statewide figure sums all 11 zones at the same timestamp.
Fuel mix New York Independent System Operator real-time fuel mix, mis.nyiso.com/public/csv/rtfuelmix/ Snapshot, every 10 min; source updates about every 5 min Statewide, not city-specific — the grid does not track electrons by borough. "Dual fuel" units burn gas or oil; the category is reported as the system operator labels it, without editorial regrouping.

Private sensor networks: what a sweep found

Several tiles already come from privately run networks with public feeds: adsb.lol (volunteer aircraft-transponder receivers), aisstream.io (volunteer ship-transponder receivers), Citi Bike (Lyft's dock telemetry) and the scooter operators Veo, Lime and Bird. A July 2026 sweep, rechecked in November 2026, of other private networks found:

  • Sensor.Community (citizen air-quality sensors, key-free and open): in November 2026 only one sensor reported inside the five boroughs, too few to summarize. Worth rechecking as the network grows.
  • PurpleAir (the largest citizen air-sensor network, with real density in the city): the API requires a key that cannot be published in page code. It could join the 10-minute snapshot if a key were added as a repository secret.
  • Raspberry Shake (citizen seismographs): its station list names about a dozen in and around the city, but none was publishing live data in November 2026; the professional Lamont-Doherty network covers the same ground on the board.
  • Weather Underground and Netatmo (thousands of private weather stations in the city): both key-gated, and the airport-grade stations on the board are the better instruments anyway.
  • SONYC (the New York University noise-sensor project): its public data releases are historical archives, not a live feed.
  • Blitzortung (a volunteer network of lightning-detection receivers, including around New York): live strike maps on its own site, but the project restricts raw data to participating members — no public API to relay.
  • BirdCast (Cornell and Colorado State's reuse of the weather-radar network to count migrating birds): a live nightly migration dashboard in season, but no documented public API. Worth revisiting when fall migration starts in August.
  • Phone-derived foot traffic (Placer.ai, SafeGraph, Unacast and kin): these firms build crowd estimates from de-identified location pings collected by mobile-app software, and sell them. A July 2026 check found no anonymous public interface — Placer.ai's API and "free tools" all sit behind accounts and contracts, and Google's per-place "popular times" busyness data has no official API at all. The closest public, sensor-based crowd measures are already on the board: subway trains and bus loads, dock telemetry and the bike and pedestrian counters.

What is not here, and why

Street flood sensors (FloodNet): real, running, and not readable live. FloodNet's ultrasonic sensors hang over more than 400 flood-prone streets and keep measuring. Until November 2026 this page counted how many were reading water on the roadway, from the raw real-time readings (read through FieldKit, the platform FloodNet's own dashboard runs on). FloodNet said in August 2026 that its public real-time data comes from a deprecated endpoint: those readings skip the project's full filtering stages, it will not stay up much longer and no public replacement is planned. The filtering matters because the sensors' worst errors look like the most dramatic floods. A stuck sensor holds a flat "flood" for hours; an ultrasonic echo doubles a reading; in a July 2026 check, one month of the raw real-time data showed more gauges above two feet than five years of FloodNet's published flood records. The checked data does exist, in two places a live page cannot use: NYC Open Data, about two weeks behind because people review and label each event, and a pre-labeled real-time service FloodNet provides only to city agencies. FloodNet plans to hand the network to the Department of Environmental Protection in March 2027, so this could change. The page keeps a note where the tile was. For the published history, see Where the streets flood.

This board aims to include every public sensor network with a live, public interface. The notable ones that don't qualify, checked as of July 2026:

  • Department of Transportation street activity sensors. The Numina-based sensors now at roughly 100 locations count pedestrians, cyclists and vehicles continuously. The city owns the data and says datasets "will be available via NYC Open Data," but as of this writing there is no public real-time interface — only press releases. When it lands, it belongs here.
  • New York State's 511NY (state roadway cameras and incident feeds) requires a registered key. It could join the board's 10-minute snapshot if a key were added as a repository secret. (The state Mesonet's weather stations are now on the board; see above.)
  • Other feeds requiring a private API key in public page code: PurpleAir's air sensors and premium aircraft feeds. (AirNow's API needs a key too, but its public files do not, which is how its readings reach the board.) (Bus Time also requires a key, but a public rate-limited one already circulates, so buses made the board.)
  • Indoor heat sensors (the Department of Housing Preservation and Development's Heat Sensors Program): real sensors in apartments of buildings with chronic heat violations, reporting temperatures all winter — but the readings go only to the agency and the building owner. The only public artifact is the annual list of buildings selected for the program. The city's other temperature-sensor experiment, the 475-device Hyperlocal Temperature Monitoring network from the Cool Neighborhoods initiative, stopped collecting in October 2019 and survives only as an archive.
  • Gunshot-detection microphones (ShotSpotter/SoundThinking): the police department does not publish sensor activations, live or delayed. A July 2026 check of the city's open-data portal found zero ShotSpotter datasets; the closest public data is the police department's historic shooting-incident file, which is compiled from reports, not from the sensors.
  • Noise sensors, the board's biggest gap: a July 2026 check found no live public noise feed anywhere in the city — zero noise-sensor datasets on the open-data portal, the SONYC academic network dormant with historical archives only and the Department of Environmental Protection's meters used for enforcement rather than publishing. The one live-ish exception: the Port Authority's roughly 30 airport noise monitors around Kennedy, LaGuardia and Newark can be watched (on about a 20-minute delay) in its WebTrak viewer, but there is no documented public API behind it.
  • Groundwater wells: the United States Geological Survey operates real-time monitoring wells on Long Island, but a July 2026 query found zero transmitting inside the five boroughs — the city's own wells report on a lag or not at all.
  • Other sensor networks with no public interface: the city's traffic-signal detection loops, Con Edison's grid telemetry (its outage map has no stable public feed) and the Department of Environmental Protection's water-quality instruments (samples are published on a delay, not live).
  • PATH train positions: PATH publishes next-arrival boards, which the PATH tile counts, but not where each train is, so PATH trains are not on the map.
  • Anything that identifies people. Everything on the board measures water, air, vehicles in aggregate or infrastructure.

Confidence notes

  • Readings are relayed exactly as published, with unit conversions only (Celsius to Fahrenheit, meters to feet, kilometers per hour to miles per hour). High confidence in the relay; the sensors themselves carry the usual instrument error.
  • Derived numbers (averages, counts, percentage shares) are simple arithmetic over the latest readings, described on each tile. Medium-high confidence; the main risk is a source publishing a bad value, which this page does not attempt to detect beyond freshness checks.
  • The air-quality headline is a measurement from the state's monitors. The model figure beside it is an estimate; treat it as context. If the monitors' reading can't be loaded, the model takes the headline and the tile says so.
  • This page is for curiosity and situational awareness, not safety-critical decisions.
